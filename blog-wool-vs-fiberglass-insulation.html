Wool vs. Fiberglass Insulation

Insulation is hidden once the walls close, but you live with it for decades. Here's how natural sheep's wool compares with the industry-standard fiberglass batt.

← All articles3 min read

Fiberglass is the most common insulation in American homes because it's inexpensive, widely available and familiar to every crew. Sheep's wool is a niche product in the U.S., but it has real advantages for clients who prioritize natural materials. Neither is the right answer for every wall, so it's worth understanding the trade-offs.

Thermal performance

Per inch, wool and fiberglass batts perform in a similar range, roughly R-3.5 to R-4.3 depending on the product and density. In practice, installation quality matters more than the material. Gaps, compression and voids around wiring reduce real-world performance far more than the difference between the two.

Moisture handling

This is where wool stands out. Wool fibers can absorb and release a meaningful amount of water vapor without losing much insulating value, which helps buffer humidity swings inside a wall assembly. Fiberglass doesn't absorb moisture, but it loses performance when wet and can hold moisture against framing. Neither material replaces good water management in the wall, but wool is more forgiving.

Health and installation

Fiberglass fibers irritate skin, eyes and lungs during installation, so crews wear protective gear. Most major brands have moved to formaldehyde-free binders, which is a meaningful improvement over older products. Wool is pleasant to handle and contains no glass fibers. Quality wool insulation is typically treated against moths and pests, often with borates. Ask what treatment a product uses if that matters to you.

Sound and comfort

Wool's density and fiber structure make it a good acoustic insulator, useful in bedrooms, home offices and media rooms. Fiberglass also helps with sound, but wool is often noticeably better.

Cost and availability

Wool costs several times more than fiberglass and has fewer suppliers, so lead times need planning. Many clients use wool selectively, in bedrooms and interior walls where they spend the most time, and use other proven products elsewhere.

Our take

If natural materials matter to you and the budget allows, wool is an excellent choice, especially in sleeping areas. If not, a well-installed formaldehyde-free product in a well-detailed wall is still a good outcome. The bigger mistake is focusing on the insulation and ignoring air sealing and moisture control.
