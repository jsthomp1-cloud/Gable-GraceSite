Designing a Home Sauna

A home sauna is one of the most-used wellness spaces we plan. Getting it right comes down to the type of heat, the materials, the ventilation and where it lives in the house.

← All articles3 min read

Whether it's a corner of a primary bath, a converted closet, or a standalone cabin by the pool, a sauna should feel like a retreat and be built to handle heat and humidity safely for years.

Traditional or infrared?

Traditional (Finnish) saunas heat the air, typically to around 160–195°F, with an electric or wood-burning stove and rocks. Pouring water on the rocks creates bursts of steam called löyly. Infrared saunas use panels that warm the body more directly at lower air temperatures, often 120–150°F. They heat up faster and use less power. Some clients choose a hybrid with both. If wireless and electrical exposure matters to you, look for infrared heaters with published low-EMF test data.

Choosing the location

Indoors, a sauna works best near a shower so you can cool off between rounds. Outdoors, it pairs naturally with a pool, cold plunge or garden. Either way, plan the path from sauna to shower or plunge so it feels easy.

Wood and materials

Interiors should use untreated, low-resin softwoods that stay comfortable to touch when hot. Western red cedar, hemlock, spruce and aspen are common choices. Thermally modified wood is a durable, chemical-free option. Avoid finishes on benches, since they can off-gas when heated.

Vapor control and ventilation

A traditional sauna needs a properly installed vapor barrier, usually foil-faced, behind the wood lining to protect the surrounding walls. Ventilation matters for comfort and safety: an intake low near the heater and an exhaust high on the opposite side keeps air fresh. Heater manufacturers specify the details, and they should be followed exactly.

Electrical and planning

Most electric sauna heaters need a dedicated 240-volt circuit sized to the heater, so plan for it early, especially in a remodel where panel capacity may be limited. Lighting should be rated for heat and moisture, and warm, dim light makes the space feel restful.

Design details that make it feel special

Tiered benches, a window to a garden view, a backrest you can lean on, and a door with tempered glass all improve the experience. Small touches, like a hook for towels and a place for water, are what make a sauna feel finished.
