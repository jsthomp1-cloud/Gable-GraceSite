What Makes a Home Healthy?

A healthy home isn't a single product or certification. It's a series of decisions about air, moisture, water, materials and light, made early and carried through to the finish.

← All articles3 min read

"Healthy home" gets used loosely, so it helps to define it. We use it to mean a house that keeps the air you breathe clean, keeps moisture where it belongs, delivers clean water, avoids unnecessary chemical exposure, and supports good rest. None of that requires giving up beauty. It does require thinking about what goes into the walls, not just what goes on them.

1. Air you can trust

People spend most of their time indoors, and indoor air can carry more pollutants than outdoor air: off-gassing from finishes and furniture, cooking byproducts, dust and moisture. Modern homes are built tighter for energy efficiency, which is a good thing, as long as fresh air is brought in deliberately. That usually means mechanical ventilation, such as an energy recovery ventilator (ERV), paired with good filtration on the HVAC system.

2. Moisture under control

Moisture is behind most of the problems people associate with unhealthy houses, mold in particular. Controlling it starts with the building envelope: flashing, drainage planes, vapor control matched to the climate, and keeping materials dry during construction. Inside, the goal is steady relative humidity, generally in the 30–50% range, which in a humid region often means dedicated dehumidification.

3. Clean water

Municipal water is treated to safety standards, but it still carries disinfectants and can pick up other things on the way to your tap. Well water varies even more. A home water test tells you what you're actually dealing with, and the filtration plan should follow from that result rather than from a sales brochure.

4. Materials that don't add to the load

Paints, adhesives, sealants, cabinetry, flooring and insulation can all release volatile organic compounds (VOCs), especially when new. Choosing low- and zero-VOC products, solid wood over certain composites, and natural materials such as wool, lime plaster and stone reduces what you're living with from day one.

5. Light and rest

Natural daylight, warm and dimmable evening lighting, and quiet, dark bedrooms all support sleep. Some clients also want to reduce wireless exposure in sleeping areas with hardwired internet and Wi-Fi switches. These choices are easy to plan for and hard to add later.

Where to start

If you can only prioritize a few things, start with moisture and ventilation. They affect everything else. Then choose finishes and materials, and test your water before choosing filtration. Most importantly, decide on these things during design, not during construction, when changes cost the most.
